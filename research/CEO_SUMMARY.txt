AI PHONE SERVICE - EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Prepared by Tony Hadad | September 3, 2026


THE PROPOSAL

Launch a new SMRT phone product for our laundry and dry-clean customers, sold
under our own brand. It does two things. First, it records and analyzes every
call a store takes, and gives the owner a dashboard: missed calls, what callers
asked about, price-shoppers who did not convert, keyword trends, staff handling
quality. Second, it answers the phone after hours with an AI agent that handles
common questions, quotes prices, checks order status and captures leads.

We recommend building this on CallTrackingMetrics (CTM) rather than building it
ourselves, launching with a 10-store pilot that costs roughly $2,200 total and
requires no engineering work.


WHY CTM

CTM is a mature call tracking platform that added an AI voice agent. It covers
both halves of our product in one system, and it is built for exactly our
situation - a company reselling to many small customers under its own brand.

The decisive factor is how it charges. CTM's Marketing Pro plan is $179 a month
plus $65 a month for white labeling. That is $244 a month total, flat, with
unlimited customer sub-accounts. Every competing platform we evaluated charges
per customer instead. GoHighLevel charges $50 to $97 per sub-account, which at
500 stores is $30,000 to $53,500 a month. Vapi and Retell have no native white
labeling at all and require a second vendor bolted on at roughly $25 per
customer per month. Because our customers are numerous but each uses very few
minutes, per-customer pricing is punishing and flat pricing is not.

The white labeling is real. The system runs on our domain, carries our logo,
sends system emails from our address, and has white-labeled login and help
pages. We set what customers pay and keep the difference.

CTM also has an open API covering accounts, sub-accounts, numbers, calls,
recordings, transcriptions, agents, reports and webhooks. That matters for two
reasons. It means we can onboard a store without anyone ever touching CTM's
interface, and it means we can build our own SMRT dashboard on top of their
platform - so customers see our product, not a reskinned version of theirs.


WHY NOT BUILD IT OURSELVES ON TWILIO

To be precise about this, because the number is counterintuitive: Twilio is
cheaper per store, not more expensive. Running the product on our own Twilio
stack costs about $38 per store per month against CTM's $62.

The problem is what it takes to get there. Building it requires roughly
$200,000 of engineering and four to six months before the first call connects,
plus ongoing hosting and 24/7 on-call - a phone product has phone-product uptime
expectations, and if the after-hours agent fails at 2am the store's line is dead
and they hear about it from a customer.

At 100 stores, building saves $843 a month, which never pays back the
investment. At 300 stores it saves $6,291 a month and pays back in 32 months.
At 500 stores it saves $11,739 a month and pays back in 17 months.

So the honest position is that building is the right long-term answer and the
wrong first move. We should build it - just not before we have proven the market
and know our real call volumes.

The plan preserves that path deliberately. We build our own dashboard on CTM's
API in year one, and swap CTM's backend for Twilio at around 300 stores. Because
the customer-facing product is already ours by then, the swap is invisible to
them.


THE MONEY

At a $199 per month price point:

- Cost to serve on CTM is about $62 per store per month at mid call volume
- Gross margin is about 69%, and it stays at 69% at any scale because CTM's
  cost is almost entirely variable
- At 500 stores that is roughly $99,500 a month in revenue against $31,000 in
  platform cost
- Building on Twilio would raise margin to about 81%, which is the reason to
  migrate eventually

The $199 price is validated by the market. CallRail, the best-known competitor
for the monitoring half, charges $50 to $195 a month for less - they have no
after-hours answering at all. We would sit at their top tier and offer more.

This also fits our existing business. It sells to the same owner who already
buys our marketing package, on the same invoice, with no new sales motion.


THE STRATEGIC ANGLE

We already run Google Ads for many of these clients. Putting tracking numbers on
each campaign turns phone calls into attributed conversions, which means our
marketing package can finally prove return on ad spend for calls, not just
clicks. That is CallRail's entire business, and we are already sitting on both
halves of it.

More importantly, we can do something no competitor can. Because SMRT holds the
order and route data, we can tell a store owner whether a specific caller
actually placed an order in the following week, and whether they were a new or
existing customer. A generic call tracking platform cannot see any of that.
That is the part of the product that is genuinely ours, and it is what stops an
owner from buying a $49 AI receptionist off the internet instead.


RISKS AND OPEN ITEMS

Call volumes are estimated, not measured. We could find no reliable published
data on how many calls a dry cleaner takes. Every cost figure above rests on an
assumption anchored to CallRail's included-minute allotments. The pilot exists
partly to measure this, and pricing should not be locked until it does.

Four commercial questions with CTM are unresolved and two of them are five-
figure swings: their real per-minute rate at our volume (published as a range
from $0.05 to $0.08), whether the AI agent's included minutes are 60 or 250 per
month, whether 3,000 included transcription minutes are per account or per
sub-account, and whether AI agents can be deployed programmatically across
hundreds of stores or must be built one at a time by hand. That last one is the
difference between a configuration push and a hiring plan.

Text messaging carries a hidden onboarding cost. Federal rules require a
separate carrier registration for each business, tied to that business's EIN,
and we cannot register clients under SMRT's EIN. Five hundred stores means five
hundred registrations. This applies equally whether we use CTM or build on
Twilio, so it does not change the recommendation, but we suggest launching
without SMS to keep it off the critical path.

Legally, we ship inbound only. Federal rules treat AI voices on outbound calls
as robocalls, at $500 to $1,500 per call with no cap. Inbound calls the customer
initiates carry a fraction of that risk. We also need a recording and AI
disclosure announcement on every call, which we will make non-configurable by
the store owner.

One vendor caution: CTM's terms of service explicitly extend to white-labeled
versions, their mobile app is CTM-branded, and their agency subscription
continues even after all sub-accounts are cancelled. None are dealbreakers, and
building our own dashboard closes the branding gaps, but they should be
understood before signing.


RECOMMENDATION

Approve a 10-store pilot on CTM. Cost is approximately $2,200 for three months
with no engineering commitment. The pilot proves demand, measures real call
volumes, and produces a working demo we can sell from.

If it performs, we scale on CTM through year one while building the SMRT
dashboard and data pipeline on their API. We revisit moving to our own Twilio
backend at around 300 stores, at which point the migration is invisible to
customers and the margin improves from 69% to 81%.

The reason to start now rather than build first is straightforward: 69% gross
margin from day one is a real business, and waiting six months and $200,000 for
81% is neither obviously better nor less risky.
